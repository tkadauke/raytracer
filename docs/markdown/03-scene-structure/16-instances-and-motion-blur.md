# 16. Instances and motion blur

A primitive's geometry — a sphere, a torus, a tessellated mesh
— is expensive to build, expensive to store in memory, and
constant for the lifetime of the scene. A scene with a forest
of trees, a field of asteroids, or a chair with four legs needs
the *same geometry* drawn many times at different positions and
orientations. The codebase's mechanism for that is
[`Instance`](../../../include/render/primitives/Instance.h):
one shape, $N$ transforms, no geometry duplication.

This chapter is also where motion blur lives, because
`Instance` is the primitive that carries it. A per-instance
velocity vector turns the static instance into a moving target
the camera sees as a smear, integrated over the shutter time.

By the end of this chapter you should know:

- how `Instance` wraps a primitive in a 4×4 transform without
  copying the underlying geometry,
- the four-matrix dance that makes per-ray transformation
  efficient (revisited from chapter 2),
- the per-instance velocity vector that drives motion blur,
- how the renderer integrates over shutter time using a sampled
  `timeSample` per ray.

## 16.1 Why we need `Instance`

Without instancing, each appearance of a primitive in the scene
needs its own geometry. A scene with 1000 spheres, each at a
different position, needs 1000 separate `Sphere` primitives
because each has a different center. That's wasteful only if
you write the scene from a hand-edited list — typing 1000 lines
of `Sphere(...)` is the kind of thing nobody does in practice.

It becomes wasteful in two specific cases. First, when the
geometry is *expensive*: a tessellated mesh from a [PLY](../appendix/a-glossary.md#p) file is
megabytes; a 1000-instance forest with that mesh would be
gigabytes if every tree carried its own copy. Second, when the
geometry is *generated by an algorithm*: a procedural city of a
million windows shouldn't materialize a million copies of the
window mesh just because each window goes at a different
address.

`Instance` solves both. The geometry lives once, and the
instance carries only the 4×4 transform that places it. Two
spheres at different positions are two `Instance`s wrapping the
same `Sphere`, with two different `setMatrix` calls.

## 16.2 The transform contract

The contract `Instance` enforces: the wrapped primitive's
geometry is defined in *local space*, and the `setMatrix` call
specifies the *local-to-world* transform that places the
instance in the scene. At intersect time, the renderer
transforms the ray into the instance's local space, runs the
wrapped primitive's intersect, and transforms the resulting
hit-point data back to world space.

The math is in
[chapter 2 §2.6](../01-foundations/02-matrices-and-transforms.md#2-6-the-four-matrix-dance-instance),
walked through line by line. The summary is that
`Instance::setMatrix(M)` precomputes four matrices:

- `m_pointMatrix = M` — for transforming hit-point world
  positions back to world space after intersection.
- `m_originMatrix = M⁻¹` — for transforming the world-space
  ray origin into local space.
- `m_directionMatrix = upper-3×3(M⁻¹)` — for transforming the
  ray direction into local space.
- `m_normalMatrix = transpose(m_directionMatrix)` — for
  transforming hit-point normals back to world space, in the
  inverse-transpose form non-uniform scale needs.

The chapter 2 widget shows these in action. Once the four
matrices are precomputed, the per-ray cost is one matrix
multiply per direction transform plus a normalization. The
wrapped primitive — a unit sphere at the origin, say — is
oblivious to the instancing; it just sees a ray in *its*
coordinate frame.

## 16.3 The classical wins

A few canonical scenes show why `Instance` is worth the
abstraction.

A **forest of trees**: load one tessellated tree mesh from PLY,
then create 200 `Instance`s wrapping it, each with a randomly-
generated translation, rotation around the up axis, and
non-uniform scale (to vary tree height). The geometry stays at
its file-load size; the scene cost grows linearly with the
instance count, not with the geometry-times-instance-count.

An **asteroid field**: load a few asteroid mesh variants, then
create thousands of instances wrapping them with random
positions and orientations. The same instance-count-only
scaling.

A **regular crystal lattice**: 6×6×6 unit cubes at integer
positions form a 216-instance solid. Each instance is the same
unit cube; only the translation differs.

The mathematical operation in each case is the same: one
geometry, $N$ transforms, $N$ instances. The scene-graph cost
scales with $N$, not with $N$ × (geometry cost).

## 16.4 Velocity and motion blur

A real camera's shutter is open for some non-zero duration, and
anything that moves during that duration appears blurred. A
moving car has tail-streaked rear lights; the wheels of a
walking dog blur against the background; a fastball blurs
between pitcher and catcher. **Motion blur** is the rendering
technique for reproducing this.

The codebase models motion blur per-instance. Each `Instance`
carries a `m_velocity` vector with the convention:

> The instance is at its `setMatrix` position at `timeSample =
> 0`, and at `position + velocity` at `timeSample = 1`.

`timeSample` is a per-ray scalar that the camera generates
from the sampler's stream API
([chapter 10 §10.4](../02-ray-rendering/10-sampling-and-anti-aliasing.md#10-4-two-access-patterns-sets-and-streams)).
Each primary ray gets a fresh `timeSample` drawn uniformly from
$[0, 1]$, representing the "instant of the shutter time" that
ray sees. With many samples per pixel, the rays for a given
pixel cover the shutter duration, and an averaged-over-samples
result shows the moving instance smeared over the trajectory it
took during the shutter.

instance's trajectory into the per-pixel blur:

The implementation is in
[`Instance::intersect`](../../../src/render/primitives/Instance.cpp).
With zero velocity, the routine takes the static fast path —
the same one chapter 2 covers — and skips any motion-blur
math. With a non-zero velocity, the routine constructs an
*adjusted* origin matrix that accounts for the instance's
shift at `state.timeSample`:

```cpp
// src/render/primitives/Instance.cpp
Vector3d shift = m_velocity * state.timeSample;
auto pointMatrixAtTime = Matrix4d::translate(shift) * m_pointMatrix;
```

The math is the same as the static case with one extra
translation: the instance, viewed at this `timeSample`, lives
at the original position plus the shift. The rest of the
matrix dance proceeds unchanged.

## 16.5 The bounding box has to expand

There's one more piece to motion blur. The [BVH](../appendix/a-glossary.md#b) traversal from
[chapter 15](15-spatial-acceleration.md) prunes by [AABB](../appendix/a-glossary.md#a)
intersection: if the ray misses the AABB, the traversal skips
the subtree. If the instance moves between `timeSample = 0`
and `timeSample = 1`, the *static* AABB doesn't enclose every
position the instance visits during the shutter, and the BVH
might prune a ray that would actually hit the moving instance.

The fix is to expand the instance's reported AABB to enclose
its full trajectory. The override is short:

```cpp
// src/render/primitives/Instance.cpp (calculateBoundingBox)
auto bbox = m_primitive->boundingBox();
bbox = bbox.transformed(m_pointMatrix);
if (m_velocity != Vector3d::null()) {
  bbox = bbox.unionWith(bbox.translated(m_velocity));
}
return bbox;
```

The static AABB and the at-`timeSample = 1` AABB get
union-ed. The result is the smallest box containing both; any
ray that hits the instance at any `timeSample` in $[0, 1]$ has
to enter this expanded box. The BVH traversal then includes
the moving instance in any ray that the expanded box overlaps,
even if the *static* box would have been missed.

## 16.6 Sampling the shutter

The motion-blur quality depends on how many samples per pixel
cover the shutter. With one sample, every ray sees the instance
at one moment of the shutter time, and the result is the
static instance at that moment — no blur. With many samples,
the rays cover the shutter densely, and the average is the
blurred image.

The dimension-independence question from
[chapter 10 §10.5](../02-ray-rendering/10-sampling-and-anti-aliasing.md#10-5-the-thin-lens-sampler-interaction)
applies here too. The pixel-offset dimension and the
`timeSample` dimension should be drawn from *independent*
sample sets — using correlated samples would couple a ray's
sub-pixel position to its shutter time, producing artifacts
where one corner of a pixel always sees one moment of the
shutter.

The `SampleStream` API provides this independence. Each call
to `stream.next()` pulls from a fresh sample dimension, so
the camera draws the pixel-offset and `timeSample` (and, for
thin-lens, the lens-disc sample) from non-correlated streams.

## 16.7 Exercises

1. Construct a scene of 1000 instances of a single 10-MB mesh.
   Estimate the total memory cost of the scene. Compare to the
   memory cost of having 1000 separate copies of the mesh.
2. The velocity vector is in *world space* — the instance
   translates by `velocity` worth of distance over the shutter.
   What changes if the velocity were in *local space* instead?
   What scenes would each model handle better?
3. Read the BVH AABB expansion in
   `Instance::calculateBoundingBox`. Predict what happens to
   BVH traversal cost in a scene of 100 stationary instances
   plus one fast-moving instance. Why?
4. Set the camera's view-plane sampler to a single-sample
   `RegularSampler` and render a scene with a fast-moving
   `Instance`. What does the rendered output look like, and
   why? Then switch to a 16-sample `JitteredSampler`. What
   changes?
